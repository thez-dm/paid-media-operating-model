Application · one system, end to end

Most accounts spend. Good ones learn. I'd build one that remembers.

The question asks what I've built. The honest answer is that anything I could describe from a previous role would be a period piece – built against bidding controls that no longer exist, on a stack that has since been rebuilt twice.

So I'm answering the harder version: the system I'd build now, in the first 90 days, and the outcome I'd sign up to be judged on.

The question

Describe an AI-powered system, workflow, or automation you've personally built to support account targeting, campaign testing, or paid media optimisation. What problem did it solve, and what was the measurable outcome?

One system, end to end: four components in the order they have to be built. Brand-agnostic on purpose – no product, budget, vertical or platform. That's the part of the work that transfers.

01 · The problem

The account has no memory, and no standard of proof.

Most paid accounts are run on recall and reaction: log in, read what the platform chose to surface, adjust, move on. It works, in the sense that the account keeps running. What it doesn't do is accumulate.

Two costs follow, and both are invisible on a dashboard. The first is that the intelligence lives in one person's head – so it leaves when they do, and every handover starts the learning curve again. The second is that without a written standard of proof, the account makes the same two errors forever.

Error one – believing a hot streak

A keyword, audience or creative with one conversion off twenty clicks looks like a star and means nothing. Budget follows it. The regression to the mean arrives three weeks later, by which point nobody remembers the decision that caused it.

Error two – killing a winner in a quiet fortnight

Keeping a loser alive is visible and embarrassing. Pausing something that was working is silent and permanent – a paused line item never gets the chance to prove the decision wrong. The expensive mistake is the one nobody catches.

Automated bidding made it worse. There are no bids left to nudge on any platform: the work moved upstream, into what we write, structure, value and feed back. Most operating models never moved with it. Teams kept the daily rhythm of an account they no longer control, and lost the discipline of the one they do.

Automation amplifies a strategy. It does not supply one.

02 · The system

Four components, in the order they have to be built.

The order is the argument. The failure mode is inverting it: build the agent first, then discover it has nothing true to reason about.

COMPONENT 01 – PREREQUISITE

A context document a machine can be held to

The bottleneck in account automation isn't engineering. The plumbing is mostly solved and an assistant will write most of it. What can't be installed is context – everything about this business a model cannot infer: what good looks like here, what we never say, which trade-offs are ours to make, what a lead is actually worth at each stage. Without it, an agent is an intern with terrifying confidence.

So the first artefact isn't code. It's a written specification of what good means in this account, precise enough that a system can be checked against it:

  • One primary conversion action, and a written value for every funnel stage behind it – so the machine optimises toward money rather than toward events.
  • What each channel is actually for, written down before a euro moves between them.
  • The claims we're allowed to make and the ones we're not, with the proof attached to each.
  • The economics: what we can pay for an opportunity, and what we'd never pay.
  • What the account will deliberately not do this quarter.
Why first: everything below is a machine checking reality against a definition. With no definition, there is nothing to check – only activity to describe.
COMPONENT 02 – THE CLIMB

Automate the reading before the reacting

Account automation is a ladder, not a switch: check, chart, change, cognition. Nobody skips a rung successfully – each rung is the guardrail for the one after it. Where a given account starts is a finding from the first audit, not an assumption. The order never changes.

The rule: prove each rung is boring before building the next. A loop built on an account that lies just reaches the wrong conclusion faster, and with more confidence.
COMPONENT 03 – THE STANDARD

Two evidence rules, made mechanical

This is what stops the system industrialising the two errors above. Both are enforced in code rather than remembered in a meeting.

  • Nothing gets believed on a hot streak. Until a line item has real spend behind it, the account's own track record speaks for it. One lucky week moves nothing; a consistent month moves money.
  • Nothing gets switched off without proof. The bar for a destructive change is deliberately higher than the bar for a reversible one, because the two mistakes are not symmetric. A pause guard sits on anything that can't be undone.

Directional at the strategy level, rigorous at the destructive one. I'd rather know what's likely true across a hundred accounts than pretend to know exactly what worked in one – but likely true is not a good enough reason to switch something off.

Why AI raises the stakes: a system that proposes fifty changes a week without an evidence standard isn't an optimisation engine. It's a very fast way to churn the account.
COMPONENT 04 – THE MEMORY

Every change leaves a trace

The cheapest component to build, and the first one teams drop. The compounding lives here. Every change – proposed by a script, an agent or a person – is written down with what we did, why we did it, what we expected, and what actually happened.

That record is the difference between an account that's smart and an account that got lucky. It's also the only thing that makes the fourth rung possible: a system can't reason about an account whose history it can't read.

The real test: can a new person read six months of the account's decisions and understand why it looks the way it does? If not, the intelligence is still in someone's head.

03 · The gate

Nothing applies itself.

Whether a change comes from a script, an agent or from me, it arrives the same way: as a short card with the evidence attached. It gets approved, questioned, or sent back. The format should follow how a team already works. The fixed part is that a human signs.

Consolidate two campaigns below signal threshold
Awaiting sign-off
Change
Merge the two lowest-volume campaigns into the parent structure; keep market separation, drop the function split.
Why
Neither campaign has cleared the conversion volume its bidding strategy needs in eight weeks. Both are learning on noise, and the split is costing us signal we're paying for.
Evidence
Eight-week conversion counts against the platform's stated learning threshold; the reporting split that the merge would lose, and where else it's recoverable.
How we'll know
Cost per qualified opportunity at the merged level, four weeks after the learning period ends, against the two campaigns' combined baseline.
If wrong
Reversible – the split can be rebuilt. Filed either way, with the result.

Illustrative – the shape of the judgement, not the specifics. A proposal that can't fill those five rows isn't ready to be a decision.

04 · The split

What the machine takes, and what stays ours.

The line isn't drawn by capability – a model can draft anything. It's drawn by accountability: the we keep column is what we'd have to defend, and defending it is the job.

Automation takes

Campaign construction, naming and hygiene

Budget pacing, negatives and exclusions

Query mining, clustering and first-pass keyword changes

Copy variant generation and first-pass translation

Statistical validation, backtesting, anomaly detection

Monitoring, receipts and the written record

We keep

The quarter's actual objective

Creative direction and brand voice

Audience and positioning ideation

Approving, questioning and rejecting the card

Judging platform recommendations against real economics

Deciding what the account will deliberately not do

AI is not the replacement for our creativity. You should not be delegating, full stop, your creative to AI.

05 · The outcome

I'm not claiming a number I earned somewhere else.

The question asks for a measurable outcome, and I'd rather commit to one I can be held to than borrow one from an account nobody in this conversation can audit.

The first month deliberately buys no automation at all. It buys the right to trust what the account is telling us – which is the only baseline any later number can be measured against.

DAYS 0–30

Make it honest

  • Tracking verified end to end with a real conversion, not a test fire
  • Consent configuration audited – confirm the data arriving is actually the data
  • One primary conversion action, with a written value for every stage behind it
  • Channel roles and account economics written down
  • The context document drafted – this is the deliverable, not a by-product
Done when: I can state the current cost per qualified opportunity with a number I'd defend in front of finance – and that number becomes the baseline.
DAYS 30–60

Make it legible

  • Spend joined to pipeline and revenue in a store we own
  • Check and chart only: scripts that detect and visualise, nothing that acts
  • Alarms on the silence, not only on the errors
  • First decision cards in circulation, so proposals have a shape before they have volume
Done when: the account has a shared view that survives me being on holiday, and a tracking break surfaces in under 24 hours instead of at the end of the month.
DAYS 60–90

Make it compound

  • Evidence-weighted proposals on the daily ring, still human-approved
  • The pause guard enforced on anything destructive
  • Two creative cycles run properly – hypothesis, room to resolve, written result
  • One incrementality test designed and running
Done when: every change in the account carries a written reason, and the loop has produced at least one decision that contradicted what we believed on day one.

What I'd be measured on

Two kinds of number. The first proves the system exists. The second proves it was worth building – and can only be targeted honestly once month one has produced a baseline, which is why the thresholds get set with the account in front of me rather than promised in an application.

Measurement trust
Share of conversion actions verified end to end, and time from a tracking break to it being noticed. The unglamorous one that determines whether any other number means anything.
Evidence coverage
Share of account changes that shipped with a written reason and a stated success test. Target: all of them. This is the number that tells you whether the system is real or decorative.
Decision latency
Time from a signal appearing in the account to a decision being made about it. The direct measure of what the automation actually bought.
Reversal rate
How often a change gets undone within the quarter. Rising means the evidence bar is too low. At zero, it's too high and nothing is being tested.
Cost per qualified opportunity
Against the month-one baseline and against the value we assigned – not against last month, and not against a benchmark from a different business.
Incremental lift
A geo holdout or lift test on the largest allocation decision of the quarter. Attribution tells us where to look; incrementality tells us whether the spend caused anything.

The single outcome I'd stake the ninety days on isn't a CAC figure. It's that the account can explain why it spent what it spent – every euro traceable to a decision, every decision to evidence, every result back into the next cycle. Efficiency gains follow from that, and are impossible to hold without it.

A result nobody can explain is a result nobody can repeat.
